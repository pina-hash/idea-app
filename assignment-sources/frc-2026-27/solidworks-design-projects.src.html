Module 1.1

Set up your sponsored SolidWorks

1 hr

You will: Install SolidWorks at home, join the team on 3DEXPERIENCE, and claim your free exam credits.

  1. Ask Mr. Pina for the team serial. He emails it. Never post or share it.
  2. On your own Windows computer, open solidworks.com/SEK, pick Yes (you have a serial), then Request Download.
  3. Run SolidWorksSetup.exe, pick Install on this computer, and paste the serial.
  4. Sign in to 3DEXPERIENCE with your school email and open the team's space.
  5. Claim free exam credits: make a Virtual Tester account, then fill the team form with the same email.

SolidWorks opens on your own computer without asking for a license, and your Virtual Tester account exists under your school email.

More detail
  • The team license is for Techmen members only, on personal computers only, until July 31, 2028.
  • The whole team shares 42 activations. Getting a new computer? Deactivate the old one first: Help > Licenses > Deactivate.
  • SolidWorks desktop runs on Windows only. On a Mac, use a lab computer.
  • Turn on MySolidWorks student access for its training library.
  • Exam credits cover 17 exams, from CSWA to Sheet Metal and Weldments, and expire Dec 30, 2026. Sign up now even if you test later.
  • Didn't get a 3DEXPERIENCE invite? Check spam, then tell Mr. Pina.
  • In 3DEXPERIENCE, open the FIRST Challenge Dashboard for your workspace and the extended part library.

Module 1.2

Design a name tag and a pen holder

1.5 hr

You will: Sketch, extrude, fillet, emboss text and shell walls, then print without supports.

  1. Open the Name Tag Student Guide. Model the body, the loop and your name.
  2. Extrude the text as an emboss, up to the level of the top edge. Fillet the corners.
  3. Open the Pen Holder Student Guide. Make every wall the same thickness with Shell.
  4. Lay both parts flat on their biggest face and print them on a Bambu printer.
  5. Log hours in the Techmen app.

Your tag prints with crisp letters and no supports, and the holder's walls match in thickness.

More detail
  • Both guides use flat-pack design: a part that prints flat needs no support material.
  • Nesting means fitting several parts on one print plate to print at once.

Module 1.3

Build a puzzle cube that fits

2 hr

You will: Split one master model into interlocking parts and design the clearance so they slide together.

  1. Model one 60 x 60 x 60 mm master block.
  2. Split it into 27 small 20 mm blocks (the guide shows how).
  3. Combine the small blocks into at least 5 interlocking pieces.
  4. Print one test pair first. If they will not slide, add clearance and print again.
  5. Print every piece, assemble the cube, and challenge a teammate to solve it.

All your pieces slide together into a 60 mm cube with no forcing.

More detail
  • The real lesson is fits and tolerances: a printed part is never exactly its CAD size.
  • Re-assemble the pieces in a SolidWorks assembly before you print.
That is not what I see
Pieces are too tightOffset the touching faces inward a little, then test print just that pair.
Pieces fall apart looseYou added too much clearance. Halve it on the next test.

Module 1.4

Win the spinning top contest

1.5 hr

You will: Shape mass on purpose using Mass Properties, then test the design against classmates.

  1. Design a top no wider than 2 in and no taller than 3 in, with a stem and a rounded tip.
  2. Check Evaluate > Mass Properties for its mass and center of mass.
  3. Print it, oriented for the least support.
  4. Time three spins. Change one thing, print again, and beat your time.
  5. Race the team's best tops and log hours.

Your top spins upright, and your second version beats your first version's time.

More detail
  • A flywheel on a robot shooter uses the same idea: mass far from the axis stores more spin.
That is not what I see
It wobbles and falls at onceMove the center of mass lower and onto the spin axis.
Supports scar the tipPrint it tip up, or sand the tip smooth.
Check yourself
Two tops have the same mass and start at the same speed. Top A keeps its mass in a wide rim; top B keeps it near the stem. Which spins longer?

Module 2.1

Build the flat-pack catapult

4 hr

You will: Design parts in the context of an assembly so they fit, move and repeat, with no fasteners.

  1. Read the Catapult Student Guide's design intent. Note the 7/8 in foam ball and the rubber band.
  2. Model two identical side plates, the front and back supports, and the face plate, all joined by slots.
  3. Build the assembly, then model the basket arm in context so it pivots on the plates' centerline.
  4. Print the flat parts nested on one plate; print the arm basket up, with organic supports.
  5. Assemble it with no glue or screws and launch. Log hours.

The catapult snaps together with no fasteners and the ball lands in about the same spot every launch.

More detail
  • The arm must strike the face plate at the same angle every time.
  • Ribs stiffen the strike plate and the arm.
  • This is how a robot shooter is designed: a hard stop makes every shot repeat.
That is not what I see
Slots are too tight to assembleAdd clearance to the slot width, then reprint one plate.
The arm rubs the platesCheck the arm's width against the gap in the assembly with Interference Detection.
Check yourself
Why does the design make the arm hit the face plate at the same angle every launch?

Module 2.2

Design the wind-powered saw

6 hr

You will: Design around parts you buy, prove fits with test prints, and turn rotation into sawing motion.

  1. Get the bought parts first: a 6 in fan blade, a 5/16 in dowel, two bearings and an M3 x 0.5 x 6 mm screw.
  2. Measure them, then model the base, two walls, the cam and the dowel holder around those numbers (mm).
  3. Print small test pieces for the wall dovetail, bearing hole, cam holes and holder dovetail.
  4. Change the CAD to the sizes that fit, then print the full set.
  5. Assemble it, point a fan at it, and cut a dowel. Log hours.

Wind spins the blade, the saw strokes, and the dowel gets cut.

More detail
  • Find a dovetail angle your printer makes without supports before you design the joint.
  • The cam turns rotation into a straight back and forth saw stroke.
  • This is the FRC habit: model the COTS part first, then design your part to it.
That is not what I see
The bearing falls out of its holeThe hole is too big. Use the next smaller size from your test print.
The saw stallsCheck for rubbing, then try a cam with a shorter stroke for more force.
Check yourself
You modeled the bearing hole at exactly the bearing's diameter and the print is too tight. Best next move?

Module 2.3

Make a kinetic sculpture move

5 hr

You will: Build a working mechanism with a cam, link and yoke, and prove it moves in CAD before printing.

  1. Model the butterfly wings, head and tail, flower, stem, cam, link and yoke, pin and stopper, and crank.
  2. Mate them so the assembly moves when you drag the crank.
  3. Turn the crank in CAD and watch for collisions before you print.
  4. Print the parts and snap them together with no glue.
  5. Crank it and log hours.

Turning the crank flaps the wings, in CAD and on the real print.

More detail
  • Use Move Component with Collision Detection to catch parts that hit.

Race a balloon-powered car

  4. Print every part on one plate, assemble it, and drag race.

The car rolls straight and your second version beats your first.

Module 2.5

Balance and fly a glider

3 hr

You will: Place the center of mass on purpose, export flat parts as DXF, and test fly.

  1. Model the fuselage, wing, stabilizer and four ballast pieces.
  2. Make the stabilizer about 1/5 the size of the wing.
  3. In the assembly, check Mass Properties: the center of mass belongs just under the wing.
  4. Save each flat face as a DXF and ask Mr. Pina which machine cuts your material.
  5. Assemble it, glue the ballasts, and test fly. Log hours.

The glider flies level instead of diving or stalling.

More detail
  • Cutouts in the fuselage match the wing and stabilizer exactly, with no tolerance.
  • Robots tip over for the same reason gliders stall: a center of mass in the wrong place.
That is not what I see
It noses up and stallsAdd ballast toward the nose.
It dives straight downRemove ballast from the nose.
Check yourself
Your glider climbs, slows and stalls on every throw. What fixes it?

Module 3.1

Load test a truss bridge

6 hr

You will: Design a printed truss that snaps together, spans 350 mm, and holds the most per gram.

  1. Pick a truss type from the guide (Pratt, Warren, Baltimore and more).
  2. Model each side section as one part with its chords, diagonals and verticals.
  3. Add the snap-in lateral braces, abutments and two-piece deck. Span at least 350 mm.
  4. Print nested, assemble with no glue, and weigh the bridge.
  5. Load it until it fails. Score = load held divided by bridge mass. Log hours.

The bridge snaps together, spans 350 mm, and you have its load held and its mass written down.

More detail
  • The deck is two pieces because the printer bed is smaller than the bridge.
  • Robot arms and frames are trusses too: triangles carry load with little material.
That is not what I see
The braces will not snap inAdd clearance at the snaps and support under their overhangs.
It broke at a joint, not a memberAdd material around the joints and test again.
Check yourself
A rectangular frame leans over under load. Adding one diagonal stops it. Why?
