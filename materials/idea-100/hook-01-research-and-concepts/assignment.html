Why go multi-part: a standard hook must leave an opening so the rope can slide on, and that open tip is where hooks pull open. A multi-part hook comes apart instead: open it, put the rope in, close it. Both loops can be solid, closed rings. Cutting a standard hook into pieces gains nothing and only adds a weak spot. The link drawn above is one way to do it, not the way.

The record, 436 lb, is held by a multi-part hook. Multi-part hooks are also the hardest to make. Every joint is a new place to break, and a joint that is not designed, test-printed, and fitted pulls apart below what a plain hook holds. Expect to work on it outside class time. For most students, a well-made standard hook is the better choice.

Think in 3D i

Cookie-cutter, the easy default. One sketch, one extrude, the same shape all the way through. It works, but it wastes the thickness. Start there, then shape it in all three directions. Below, orange marks the feature. These are examples: combine them or invent your own. Tap one to start your answer.

Overhangs, supports and layers i

Strength comes first, but think about the printer now, not at the end. You can tilt the part and you can use supports. Know what each support costs, and design out the ones that are not worth it.
