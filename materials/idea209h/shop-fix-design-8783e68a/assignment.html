Two major components
Both made from shop stock or scrap. Label your stock and put it on the part cart, or label it where it sits if it is too big.
One is machined
On the mill, lathe or CNC router. Each person owns their own machined part.
A real machined part
Two or more machined features beyond squaring up the stock, each its own setup or operation. A faced block with one hole is not enough.
The other major part
Made on the mill, lathe, CNC router, H2D laser or xTool MetalFab. From shop stock or scrap, never printed or bought.
Minor parts
3D prints and small bits of anything else, for supporting parts only. A 3D print is never a major part.
Fasteners from our bins
Screws, nuts, inserts and pins come from the IDEA hardware bins. Check the bins while you design: there is no ordering for this project.
One critical interface
Your two major parts meet at one critical interface you design, predict and measure: a fit, a fastened joint, a weld, a hinge or a slide. You pick the one critical dimension that decides whether it works.
Unique
Claim it on the Shop Fix Board first. Two students may take on the same problem only if their fixes are different, or if the problem is in a different place.
Teams
Up to 3, only with your teacher's OK before Checkpoint 1. A team does not cut anyone's work: every teammate designs, draws and makes their own two major parts, the same as a solo project. One on the mill, lathe or CNC router; the other on one of those or a laser. The team shares the problem, and each teammate turns in their own posts.
Sign-offs are the gate
Sign-offs are not points: they unlock the next step. Checkpoint 2 before you start CAD, Checkpoint 4 before you touch a machine. A project that breaks a rule is not signed off until it is fixed.
The remove test
Pull your machined part out of the design. If the fix still works, the machined part is decoration and does not count.

Waiting is slide time. Build your presentation in line, not the night before.

Your two major parts meet at one critical interface. You pick the critical dimension that decides whether it works, design it with a tolerance, predict what you will actually get, then measure it in your Build Log fit check.

Pin in a hole
Critical dimension: clearance = hole minus pin.
Calipers on the pin (outside jaws, three spots, keep the largest) and the hole (inside jaws, keep the smallest).
Tab in a slot
Critical dimension: clearance = slot minus tab.
Calipers on both, then push the tab to one wall and check the gap with feeler gauges at three spots.
Fastened joint
Critical dimension: the hole spacing that must match the other part, or the clearance of the screw in its hole.
Calipers center to center (measure edge to edge, then subtract one hole diameter), or try the screws through both parts.
Welded or bonded
Critical dimension: the joint gap, or the length or angle that must come out right after joining.
Calipers or a tape on the finished length; a square and feeler gauges for how far out of square.
Hinge, slide or something else
Critical dimension: the one number that decides whether it moves, holds or lines up.
Pick the measuring tool that reads it, and say which in section 07.
The shop will not hold a thousandth. This lab finds out what it can hold, with you running it. Your prediction is recorded on the board when you ask for Checkpoint 4, before you cut. Missing it is fine. Explaining the miss is the grade. Tighter is not better: give it the tolerance your fix needs, in inches with three decimals, like 0.010. For an angle, give how far out of square it is over the length, in inches.

On paper, sketch two different designs for the fix you claimed: the same job, built two different ways, like a different mechanism, layout, parts or way of holding. Not the same design at a new size.   • Every dimension needed to make each part
  • The material of each part
  • The critical interface circled: where the two major parts meet
  • Fasteners drawn in, if you use any
