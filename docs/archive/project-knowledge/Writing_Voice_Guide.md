# Writing Voice Guide

## Purpose

This document exists so that any message drafted on your behalf, email, text, GC post, Discord or GroupMe announcement, reads like you wrote it and not like an AI wrote it.

It's a different thing from the "Communication Style" section in `IDEA_instructions.md`. That section governs how Claude talks to you. This one is about how you talk to everyone else.

It's built from real material: your own typed draft to Luis Garza, edits you made when an early team email came back sounding wrong, and things you've said directly about what does and doesn't sound like you. Where a scenario is still a guess rather than a confirmed pattern, it's flagged as such rather than presented as settled.

---

## Core voice, true across every scenario

- **No em dashes, ever.** Use a hyphen or rewrite the sentence. Confirmed everywhere, formal and casual.
- **No corporate phrasing, no AI-sounding transitions.** You've said this directly. No filler, no motivational framing, nothing that reads like a template.
- **Direct and concise.** State the point. Don't pad it, don't hedge it, don't restate it. Every sentence carries new information or a decision, or it gets cut.
- **No restating what the other person just said before responding.** Answer the thing. Skip the recap.
- **No throwaway closers.** "Let me know if you have any questions" or "looking forward to hearing from you" only earns a place if it adds real information. Otherwise the message just ends.
- **Facts and decisions over open questions.** When there's a reasonable path forward, you state it. This shows up in your own outbound writing, not only in how you want Claude to respond to you.
- **No pep-talk language, even in rally-the-team writing.** Real stakes stated plainly land harder for you than motivational filler. "The gap is real. Show up." does more work than "Let's go team!"
- **Short sentences over long compound ones**, especially anything meant to be scanned fast: announcements, GC posts, texts.
- **Real problems get named plainly, not softened.** "Only 4 rows were submitted. Please come see me so we can talk about what happened" is the pattern, not "It looks like there may have been some difficulty completing the assignment."

### Quick reference: avoid vs. use

**Avoid, reads as AI-generated:**
- "I hope this email finds you well"
- "I wanted to reach out to..."
- "Furthermore" / "Moreover" / "Additionally"
- "It's important to note that..."
- "In today's fast-paced world..."
- "Let's dive in" / "At the end of the day"
- Em dashes
- "Please don't hesitate to reach out" / "I look forward to hearing from you"

**Use instead:**
- Open with the point, skip the warm-up sentence
- Hyphens for separation
- Plain caps section headers in announcements
- A direct closer if one is needed ("Reply to sign up," "Let me know") or no closer at all

## Punctuation and mechanical habits

- Hyphen as separator in place of an em dash: "Outstanding work - one of the strongest submissions in the class."
- Full sentences in email. Fragments are fine in texts, GroupMe, and quick notes.
- Section headers in team announcements: plain caps, no colon needed (SHOP HOURS, not "Shop Hours:").
- Minimal exclamation points, even when the message needs to feel urgent. Urgency comes from word choice and short sentences, not punctuation.
- Casual register (texts, quick notes) skips capitalizing the first word sometimes and drops closing punctuation on short fragments. Formal email register doesn't do this.

## Scenario breakdown

### Email to a colleague or peer-level contact
Basis: a scheduling question to a fellow teacher (Sam Hankins).
- Plain subject line stating the topic, no cleverness: "Band after-school schedule."
- Short context, then the direct ask. No throat-clearing before the question.
- Sign off with first name.

### Email to FRC leadership or a mentor relationship (Luis Garza)
Basis: a concern about FSP tech-selection timing, written by you directly.
- Plain opening. "Hello," is enough if replying inline, no forced warmth needed.
- States the problem with a clear cause-and-effect structure: since X happens, Y is the result.
- Grounds the concern in something observed, not just opinion: "I noticed... students told me..."
- Acknowledges the other side before pushing a position: "I understand X, but..."
- States your own position plainly and without hedging once the nuance is on the table.
- Ends solution-oriented. You offer to help build the fix, not just flag the problem.

### Team-wide announcements (email, Discord, GroupMe)
Basis: weekly and kickoff team communications.
- Sign off "- Mr. Pina."
- Caps section headers, short paragraphs, dash or bullet lists for facts (hours, dates, requirements).
- Direct calls to action, no cheerleading: "Read this." "Show up." "Reply to sign up."
- States real stakes plainly: "The gap is real."
- Content gets reformatted per platform rather than pasted everywhere unchanged: full detail in email, condensed and split across several short posts for GroupMe's character limit, one markdown post with headers, bold, and blockquotes for Discord.

### Google Classroom posts
- Plain text only. No markdown, no headers, no bullet symbols that won't render (already a hard rule in `IDEA_instructions.md`, and it matches the real examples on file).
- Short and factual. A grading announcement states exactly how the score was calculated and stops there.

### Grading feedback and individual student comments
- Specific, not general: the exact item, row, or concept, never "some errors."
- Positive feedback stated plainly when earned: "Outstanding work."
- Problems named directly: "Please come see me so we can talk about what happened."
- Rubric reasoning avoids third-person pronouns (he/she), phrased as factual statements about the work rather than commentary directed at the student.
- No closing filler unless it adds real information.

### Quick corrections and casual notes (texts, chat with collaborators)
- Fragment-style is fine. Full sentences aren't required.
- States the problem in as few words as possible: "too wordy."
- No exclamation points even when the correction is firm. The bluntness carries the weight on its own.
