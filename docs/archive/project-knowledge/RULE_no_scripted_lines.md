## Hard rule: no scripted lines

**Version 1.1 - 2026-08-26**

Instructor-facing materials list **topics and subtopics**. They never contain
language written for Mr. Pina to read aloud.

Prohibited unless he explicitly asks for wording:

- Quoted dialogue, blockquoted delivery text, or any sentence in his voice
- "Say this," "open with," "tell them," "use this framing"
- Suggested phrasings, transitions, or delivery notes
- Talk tracks, scripts, run-of-show narration

Required form:

- Topic headings with short noun-phrase bullets underneath
- Time allocations, sequence, and hard stops
- Facts, numbers, dates, and constraints he needs on hand
- Guards stating what to skip or not cover

The deliverable's only job is to remind him what to cover, in what order, and
when to stop. A checklist he can glance at, not a document he has to read.

### When he is not the expert on the subject

This rule was written on the premise that he already knows what to say. That
premise does not hold everywhere. Established 2026-08-26 on the IDEA209H Unit 1
calculation content, where Mr. Pina stated plainly that he is familiar with the
subject but is not an expert in it, and that he is improvising these sessions
with little or no preparation time.

The answer is **not** to start scripting him. Reading a script while improvising
is worse than improvising cold, and every prohibition above is unchanged.

The answer is to move the load off the notes and onto the artifact:

- **The sheet carries the substance.** Method, worked number, and units are
  visible on the slide, so he narrates what the room can already see rather than
  recalling anything. A sheet that needs a note to make sense is the defect, and
  the fix is to the sheet.
- **Notes carry what an expert would know and a non-expert would not.** The
  wrong answer to expect and why it looks right. The number that should come
  out. The question a student will ask, and its answer. The tangent to defer.
- **Anticipated answers are facts, not lines.** Supplying the answer to a
  question a student is likely to ask falls under "facts he needs on hand"
  above. Supplying the sentence he should answer it with does not. The test is
  whether the note is still useful if he says it in completely different words.
- **No sheet may depend on an unwritten judgment call.** Already true for Mr.
  Cosso under the Block 4 constraint. It is now equally true for Mr. Pina on
  content he is meeting cold, so the two audiences for instructor notes have
  converged and one artifact serves both.

This also applies to any material Mr. Cosso operates. Cosso gets the same
topic list, not a script.

Exception: he asks for wording by name, or the material is student-facing
copy, parent communication, or email, where the words themselves are the
deliverable.
